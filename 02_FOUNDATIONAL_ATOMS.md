# Dynamic Data — The Foundational Atoms

*Captured 2026-07-14. The design spec for what a claim must carry to be a
foundation, not just a schema.*

## Atoms vs capabilities — the separation that matters

The benchmark listed **capabilities** (things you can *do*). You only get to
choose **atoms** (things a claim *carries*); the capabilities are whatever the
atoms generate. Getting the atoms right is the whole game.

| Benchmark item | Really a… | Generated by which atoms |
|---|---|---|
| single current value | capability (resolution) | credence + context + lifecycle |
| provenance | capability (trace) | source + derivation |
| calibrated confidence | **atom** (credence) | — primitive |
| time-travel | capability | *valid-time* = one axis of **context** |
| conflict surfaced | capability | identity + proposition + context |
| change history | capability | record-time + lifecycle |

Five of the six are derived. Pick the atoms wrong and no capability layer saves
you; pick them right and capabilities you never listed fall out for free.

## The test a foundational set must pass

1. **Complete** — nothing essential is inexpressible.
2. **Minimal / orthogonal** — no atom derivable from the others.
3. **Universal** — works across every domain.
4. **Reflexive / extensible** — absorbs dimensions not yet imagined without
   breaking the core. (This is the "future data" test.)

## The eight atoms

1. **Proposition** — `subject · predicate · value/obj` (what is asserted).
2. **Identity** — *which entity the subject really is* (resolved, not a bare
   string). The most foundational: conflict, provenance, and history are
   meaningless if you can't tell two claims are about the same thing. (Classic identity problem: three email addresses, one person — same_as resolves them.)
   Implemented reflexively via `same_as` claims + a canonical resolver.
3. **Source** — who/what asserts it.
4. **Derivation** — *what it's based on* (edges to the claims/rules it came
   from), not a flat evidence string. This is the atom that gives **belief
   revision**: retract a premise and everything derived from it re-evaluates.
   It's what makes the system *learn* instead of just *log*.
5. **Credence (typed)** — confidence that can be a `point`, an `interval`, or
   **`unknown`**. A scalar `0.5` cannot distinguish *"equiprobable"* from *"I
   have no idea"* — and for AI that distinction is everything.
6. **Context** — the conditions under which the claim holds. **Time-travel is
   just one axis of this** (valid-time); so are world/reality, timeline,
   framework, and space. Truth is context-relative. Collapsing context to only
   time is a mistake.
7. **Record-time** — when it entered the system (the other half of
   bitemporality; gives history + audit).
8. **Lifecycle** — active / superseded / retracted.

From these eight, all six benchmark capabilities **plus** belief revision,
provenance chains, counterfactuals, and framework-relative truth are derivable.
That they generate more than you asked for is the sign they are the right
altitude.

## Reflexivity — the answer to "future data"

You cannot future-proof by guessing every dimension now; you will always miss
one. The only thing that survives contact with future data:

> **The set of dimensions a claim can carry is itself dynamic data.**

An attribute is a claim; a context-type is a claim; "confidence means X here" is
a claim. A dimension nobody imagined is added by *asserting new claims*, not by a
breaking migration. Same meta-circular trick that lets RDF describe RDF or Lisp
describe Lisp. Concretely in dd-core:

- Every claim has an open `dims` bag for arbitrary/future dimensions.
- A claim's subject may be *another claim's id* (meta-claim) or `predicate:<name>`
  (a claim about a dimension). So the vocabulary is editable from inside.

A minimal orthogonal **core** + an open **self-describing extension** = a
foundation. A fixed struct, however well chosen, is not future-proof.

## Map: production claim systems already implement most of this

The `dd-core` toy was deliberately minimal, but this atom set maps cleanly onto
real production claim/fact systems — see doc 01 for the validating-example
discussion of one such system reaching for the same richer set independently.

## Verdict

- The six benchmark items are good **verbs**; the foundation is in the **nouns**
  underneath — identity, context, derivation, credence — and in making the
  noun-list itself editable from inside the system.
- Nail identity + context + derivation + typed credence, plus reflexivity, and
  the primitive genuinely generalizes to any and future data. Leave them out and
  it is a nice knowledge base, not a foundation.
